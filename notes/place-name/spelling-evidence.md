# Hebrew spellings of Italian names by Italian Jews: attested evidence

Research notes, 2026-11-04. Read-only research; nothing in the project was changed.

## Labels and sources

- **VERIFIED**: I saw the form myself, either in a scan or in a digital transcription of the text (Sefaria or Hebrew Wikisource). When the only witness is a modern digital transcription, I say so, because it may carry OCR or editorial errors.
- **REPORTED**: a secondary source or search-engine summary gives the form, but I did not see it in the source.
- **UNKNOWN**: nothing found.

How reliable each source is as evidence of *Italian* Jewish usage:

Not found (UNKNOWN): **Muggia = מויא** (no Hebrew attestation anywhere I could search; the only hits for מויא are unrelated). INFERRED, unverified: Muggia is *Muja* in the local Venetian-Triestine dialect, so מויא would match the dialect pronunciation, as Casalmaggiore = קאזיל מזור does. No attestation either for Foggia, Correggio, Viareggio, Argenta, Gemona, Angelo, Luigi, Giuseppe, or Biagio. **Reggio di Calabria 1475 colophon**: I found no transcription or legible scan (the j-italy.org page has no Hebrew); UNKNOWN.

**Pattern:** there are two traditions. (a) The 16th-century Veneto writer (Maharam of Padua) writes soft g as yod alone (מייו, ייוניו), or as ז where the dialect has /z/ (קאזיל מזור). (b) From the 18th–19th centuries the usual form is ג followed by yod: גי before a vowel (גינובא, ריגיו, פורמיגיני) and גיי for Reggio (ריגייו, in Ḥida and in the 1795 ketubba, which has a diacritic on the gimel). ג' appears in Benamozegh (1862). The owner's yod-only rule is attested for the 16th-century Veneto (מייו), but not for town names in later documents.

S before a consonant and initial s, for comparison: **אגושטו** (agosto, Maharam of Padua 72:1); **פייבי"א די שאקו** (Piove di Sacco, Ḥida, Shem ha-Gedolim Gimel Kunṭres Aḥaron 5:2); **פוסמורונו** (Fossombrone, Ḥida, Nun 40:1); **טריאסטי** (Shadal).

Not found: Pisa (the modern-catalogue פיסא appears only in Krengel's additions), Asolo, Lisbona (ליסבונה, non-Italian sources only), Rosa.

**Pattern:** mixed. Voiced intervocalic s is written **ז** by northern writers (קזאלי, Norzi, Mantua; פיזארו, Shadal; קאזיל, Padua) and **ס** by others (פיסארו, Ḥazan in Rome; ציסאנו in the Marche). Initial s and s before a consonant are often **ש** in the older texts (שאקו, אגושטו), which matches the old Italian Hebrew habit of writing s as ש.

**Pattern:** Hebrew forms follow the Italian (or Venetian) pronunciation. Final -a is written **א** (אנקונא, סיניגאליא, ברישא), sometimes **ה** (פירארה, מנטובה, ויניציאה); final -e is written **י** (קזאלי, טריאסטי, פירינצי); e and i are both **י**; a long Italian a in an open syllable is often written **א** (פאדובה, קאסטיליאנו, פיסארו).

## 8b. v next to a consonant

Question: when an Italian v stands after l, r, or n (or before r/l), do Italian Hebrew texts write it ב, ו, or וו? About 30 minutes of searching (Sefaria search API, both `exact` and `naive_lemmatizer`; Hebrew Wikisource search and raw page text). The evidence is thin: only two words turned up, both with several spellings.

| Hebrew form | Italian | Source | Label |

Aside: on the same *ʿArukh* title page, Archivolti is written **ארקוולטי**. Its Italian v stands between vowels (Archi-volti), so it does not count here, but it is another וו.

**Not found** (zero hits on Sefaria and Wikisource for the spellings tried with ב, ו, and וו): Servadio, Silvera/Silveira, Salvadori/Salvatore, Calvo, Corvo, Cervo, Silvestro, Silvio, Malvano, Malvezzi, Malvasia, Benvenuto, Orvieto, Ivrea, Sanvito, conserva, polvere, inverno, salvia. No example at all of v before r or l. Italian Jewish surnames such as Servadio (= ʿOḇadya) and Cervo (= Ṣeḇi) are probably written with the Hebrew name, which would explain the gap (INFERRED). The *Seder ha-Dorot* (Lithuania, not Italy) writes «חזקיה די סילוא» for da Silva, with a single ו.

**Pattern:** after a consonant, v has no single spelling. After n, ב is the usual form in the manuscripts (Benevento: בנבנטו, ביניבינטו), and ו appears in print (בינוינטו). After l, the Venetian printers of the 1550s write both וו (אלוויזי, 1552) and ו (אלויסי, 1553). No attested example of v before r or l was found.
