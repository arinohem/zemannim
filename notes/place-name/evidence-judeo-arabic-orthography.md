## 1. Tāʾ marbūṭa

**Periods (VERIFIED, Khan 2002 p. 603).** Classical JA (10th to about 15th century) follows Arabic-script spelling ("made to correspond to the orthographic conventions of Arabic"). Early and Late JA (after the 15th century) instead follow Rabbinic Hebrew and Aramaic spelling habits and phonetic writing.

**Hebrew Wikipedia (VERIFIED, raw text).** It says ة "מקובל לתעתק הׄׄ / ה࣫" (encoded as ה + U+05C4 twice, or ה + U+08EB). It adds that in construct ת is written "לעיתים קרובות מאוד". The source is Blau, *Diqduq*, 2nd ed., 1995, p. 40. I could not read Blau, so the two-dots claim attributed to him is REPORTED.

**Absolute (non-construct) state, from Connolly §2.1.4.1 (VERIFIED).**
- Plain ה is the norm. Her summary: "This is in keeping with the graphical representation of tā' marbūṭa, which occurs in classical JA texts."
- In 15th-century letters ה is "occasionally written with two supralinear dots in imitation of its Arabic graphical form". So ה with two dots is an occasional practice, not the default.
- א for -a occurs in the 15th-century and the 18th–19th-century folk tales, for example מרא, שגרא, טבקא. It is "rare" in later letters. She reads א as "indicative of phonetic realisation".

**Construct state, Connolly §2.1.4.2 (VERIFIED).**
- Three spellings occur: ה, ה with two dots or dashes, and ת.
- The dotted ה is a "direct imitation of the graphical form" of ة. It is most common in 15th–16th-century folk tales (e.g. מדינה'' אלטיור) and absent in the 18th–19th century.
- ת (e.g. גהת אלעתל, חצרת אלולד) "has already become the norm" in 15th-century letters. In 18th–19th-century folk tales it is used "exclusively".
- She attributes ת either to pronunciation or to imitation of Hebrew practice (citing Wagner 2010: 39).

**Classical Geniza place names (VERIFIED as PGP transcriptions).**
- Spellings: אלמהדיה (many documents), סוסה (PGPID 2954, "אלמהדיה וסוסה"), אלאסכנדריה (PGPID 2893), ספאקץ (Sfax, 5717).
- Construct with ה: "קלעה חמאד" (Qalʿat Banī Ḥammād, PGPID 3674, c. 1050 letter from Tlemcen) and "קלעה געבר" (Qalʿat Jaʿbar, 3242).
- Construct with ת: "בזוילת אלמהדיה" (Zawīlat al-Mahdiyya, 1237). The same phrase appears with ה as "בזוליה אלמהדיה" in 2520.
- I found no "קלעת" in the PGP snippets. That absence is not evidence that it never occurs.

**Later Egyptian place names (VERIFIED, Connolly).**
- סכנדרייה (Alexandria, 15th-century letter, p. 54–55).
- אל לדקייה (Latakia, 18th–19th-century letter, p. 55).
- מדינה אלאטיאר / מדינה'' אלטיור (construct "city of", p. 56–57).

**Saadia, Derenbourg edition (VERIFIED, Sefaria).**
- Gen 10:30 "מן מכה … אלי אלמדינה": Mecca and Medina, both with ה.
- Gen 41:45 אלאסכנדריה.
- Construct with ה: "בחירה טבריה" (Deut 4:49) and "זוילה" (Gen 25:18).
- I saw no two-dot ה in this edition.

**Jeddah: UNKNOWN.** The PGP search for גדה gave only *ghadan* "tomorrow" and unclear personal names. I found no attestation of Jeddah.

**Modern Maghrebi print.** A Princeton catalogue record gives the Sousse imprint as "סוסה" (M. Najar, 1930/31). This is REPORTED, since it is the cataloguer's form. I found no verified modern print using final א for a place name: UNKNOWN.

## 2. Jīm, ghayn, khāʾ and the other dotted letters

**The system (VERIFIED, Connolly p. 13, citing Blau 1981: 34–5 and Blau & Hopkins 1984: 10).**
- From the Classical period, the extra letters are made by adding "a supra- or sub-linear diacritic" to a Hebrew letter: צ for ḍād, ת for thāʾ, ד for dhāl, ג for both jīm and ghayn, כ for khāʾ, ט for ẓāʾ.
- Her footnote 35 warns that the "placement (or indeed presence) of the diacritic" is not fixed.
- ghayn, khāʾ, and thāʾ were matched to the spirant allophones of ג כ ת (Blau, Hary, Khan, as cited by Connolly). The dot on צ for ḍād comes from the letters' graphic likeness.

**Jīm (VERIFIED, Connolly §2.1.2.2.1).**
- Early JA (8th–9th century papyri): unadorned ג.
- 10th–15th century: "generally recorded as a gimel with a sub-linear dot", imitating the dot of ج.
- Her own manuscript counts vary a great deal. Some 11th-century letters never mark it. Some 11th–13th-century texts use a dot above.
- A Maghrebi letter of about 1500 writes ג "without a sub- or supra-linear dot" for both jīm and ghayn, "a practice that is often noted as characteristic of late texts". 19th-century letters: no dot.
- She concludes the dot is largely graphic and inconsistent in every period.

**Ghayn (VERIFIED, Connolly §2.1.1.4).**
- 15th–16th century: a dot or dash above, used to very different extents.
- From the 17th–18th century: a dot below in some texts, none in others.
- The writer is free to choose.

**Khāʾ (VERIFIED).** A dot above כ becomes regular in 18th–19th-century literary texts. Connolly reads it as imitating the Arabic letter خ. Final ך usually stays unmarked.

**Dhāl (VERIFIED).** Connolly (footnote 43, citing Blau 1981: 35 n. 2) says the dot is "less common than is suggested by transcriptions or editions", because editors add dots themselves. In late texts it appears mainly in words of Classical Arabic origin (Khan 1992).

**Editions differ.**
- Derenbourg's Saadia on Sefaria marks jīm and leaves ghayn plain: ג̇מיע, ג̇אנב, but אלגור, אלגנם. It marks khāʾ, dhāl, ḍād, and thāʾ with U+0307 (כ̇, ד̇, צ̇, ת̇). VERIFIED.
- The PGP snippets show plain ג for jīm in "קלעה געבר". This is VERIFIED as a transcription; the manuscript itself is UNKNOWN.

**Wikipedia tables do not agree (VERIFIED).**
- Hebrew Wikipedia: ج = גׄ, غ = plain ג, no citation.
- English Wikipedia: ج = plain ג; غ = גׄ, עׄ, or רׄ.
- English Wikipedia also gives khāʾ as כׄ or חׄ, and ẓāʾ as טׄ or זׄ.

**ע̇ or ח̇: UNKNOWN.** I found no scholarly source for ע̇ as ghayn or ח̇ as khāʾ. The only ones are English Wikipedia's table (עׄ, רׄ, חׄ, uncited) and the Iraqi Judeo-Arabic dictionary title in Hebrew Wikipedia's bibliography, which I did not check.

**Shape of the mark.**
- In manuscripts it is a dot, sometimes a dash, above or below (Connolly, VERIFIED).
- Digital encodings: Hebrew and English Wikipedia use U+05C4. Derenbourg on Sefaria uses U+0307. PGP uses an apostrophe.
- I found no scholarly source prescribing a geresh-shaped stroke. Its use in modern print is UNKNOWN.

## Consequences for the app (INFERRED)

- **Final -a written א** matches Late JA phonetic writing, especially folk tales. It goes against the Classical and the documentary norm, which is ה, with ה plus two dots as an occasional graphic variant.
- **Dotted ג for ghayn** is supported in the Classical period. Writing ghayn as plain ג would also be attested.
- **Plain ג for jīm** is attested early and late. The Classical norm, as described in the scholarship, is a dotted ג for jīm (below or above). The result is that both letters can be written ג, dotted or plain, which is ambiguous. Whatever is chosen, the dot is a convention and not a fixed rule.
